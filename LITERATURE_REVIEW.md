# Literature review: bacterial wastewater treatment

Curated reading notes and published summary data. These are findings from other researchers, not experimental results from this project. Prepared 25 October 2026.

## Scope and selection

This focused narrative review covers microbial communities and aerobic granular sludge (AGS), with emphasis on full-scale municipal treatment. It is not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Publisher pages, university records, and indexed abstracts were consulted. Access limitations are identified below; no paywalled full text is claimed as read.

## Primary research

### 1. Full-scale upgrade and microbial succession

Świątczak and Cydzik-Kwiatkowska (2018) studied conversion from activated sludge to AGS. The influent included dairy wastewater, reaching up to 40% of the total; this is not a domestic-only benchmark. Reactor volume fell by approximately 30%. Total phosphorus removal was 95.1 ± 1.9%, and effluent total nitrogen changed from 19.2 ± 1.9 to 11.7 ± 0.8 mg/L. The reported 67.4% decrease in effluent COD compares before and after upgrading; it is NOT the influent-to-effluent removal efficiency.

The study links granule maturation to changes in the microbial community and highlights extracellular polymeric substances as structural contributors. This observational upgrade cannot isolate the effect of individual bacterial taxa. Access: publisher full text, abstract, and treatment-efficiency discussion. [Source 1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-017-0615-9).

### 2. Long-term granulation in municipal treatment

Ekholm et al. (2022) followed two full-scale AGS reactors for more than one year. The university abstract reports stable sludge concentrations of 6–8 g/L, with 50–80% of granules larger than 2 mm and an SVI30/SVI10 ratio of 0.9–1. These are biomass and settling characteristics, not pollutant-removal percentages. The work supports tracking microbial succession alongside physical granule properties.

For this project, record granule size, biomass concentration, and settling indicators together rather than choosing one as a complete performance measure. Access: publisher metadata and author-institution abstract; full numerical tables were not extracted. [Source 2](https://doi.org/10.1039/D2EW00653G). [Institutional record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/533345).

### 3. Bacterial communities across treatment plants

Tyagi et al. (2025 issue) examined fourteen full-scale treatment plants and samples from primary, secondary, and tertiary stages. The study concerns domestic sewage and relates bacterial community patterns to water-quality variables. Its multi-plant design is useful for thinking about site variation, but community associations should not be treated as proof that one organism causes pollutant removal. No removal percentage is extracted here. Access: indexed abstract and publisher metadata. [Source 3](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxics13010003).

## Review articles to read

### 4. Continuous-flow AGS

*Continuous flow aerobic granular sludge: recent developments and applications* (2024) reviews reactor configurations, granulation, and implementation in existing treatment plants. It is relevant when comparing batch-based research with continuous municipal treatment. Use it to locate original studies, rather than treating values compiled from different reactors as interchangeable. Access: indexed abstract and publisher search excerpt. [Source 4](https://doi.org/10.2166/wst.2024.055).

### 5. Microbial consortia and startup

Al-Gheethi et al. (2024) review AGS as a startup material, particularly for food-processing wastewater. They discuss community diversity, acclimation, and differences between source and receiving environments. The project implication is to document inoculum origin and adaptation rather than assume transferability. Industrial food-wastewater findings require separate interpretation from domestic sewage. Access: publisher full text. [Source 5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13201-024-02268-2).

## Synthesis and next research steps

The reading suggests evaluating treatment as a microbial community operating within a reactor, rather than as a single bacterial isolate acting independently. This is a project interpretation, not a result generated here.

Before comparing studies, extract wastewater composition, scale, temperature, hydraulic and solids retention times, analytical method, and sampling design. Keep missing metadata explicit. Avoid ranking technologies from the selected values: their endpoints and denominators differ. Future project data should be compared against controls and reported with independent replicate counts and uncertainty.
